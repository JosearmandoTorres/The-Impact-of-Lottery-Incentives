\section{Introduction}
% ============================================

Survey researchers have long been attentive to response rates \parencite{steeh1981},
treating them as an important indicator of survey quality
\parencite{backstrom1963, babbie1990, aday1996, rea1997}.
But the more fundamental concern is representativeness. Certain groups tend to respond
at lower rates: men refuse more often than women, and lower-income households are less
likely to participate \parencite{groves2006, bee2024}. Other attributes such as
socioeconomic status and racial minority status show variable effects depending on survey
mode, topic, and sponsor \parencite{groves2006}. When these participation gaps correlate
with the variables a survey aims to measure, nonresponse bias compromises accuracy
regardless of the overall response rate.

This problem has become more pressing as response rates have declined across all modes:
mail \parencite{casro1982}, telephone \parencite{curtin2005}, and online
\parencite{sheehan2001}. Researchers have experimented with survey length, advance
notifications, personalization, follow-up protocols, and refusal conversion strategies
\parencite{brick2013}. Among these approaches, incentives remain one of the most studied
and debated tools for increasing participation \parencite{singer2013}.

Two theoretical frameworks help explain why incentives might motivate survey
participation. The first is \textcite{cialdini1984}'s principle of reciprocity: when
someone gives us something, we feel psychologically obligated to give something back.
This obligation can be triggered by gifts, favors, or perceived concessions, and it
operates even when the initial offering was unsolicited. In the survey context, an
incentive represents a gift from the sponsor, creating a sense of debt that respondents
may settle through participation.

The second framework is leverage-saliency theory \parencite{groves2000}. This theory
proposes that different attributes of a survey request carry different ``leverage'' for
different people. Whether an attribute influences behavior depends on whether it becomes
salient during the request. Incentives, on this account, work by increasing the salience
of potential rewards for individuals who assign positive leverage to monetary gain. This
explains the inconsistent findings in the incentives literature: effectiveness depends on
both individual predispositions and what the survey request emphasizes.

Both frameworks suggest that incentives should increase participation among people who
would otherwise lack sufficient motivation to respond. If true, incentives could improve
sample representativeness by drawing in underrepresented groups. But when incentives take
the form of lotteries, additional psychological factors come into play.

Lottery incentives are appealing to researchers because they offer the possibility of
meaningful prizes at low per-respondent cost. But lotteries introduce psychological
complexity. When a researcher has a fixed budget, they can structure a lottery with many
winners of smaller prizes or fewer winners of larger prizes. The expected value can be
held constant, but the two framings may differ in their psychological appeal.

\textcite{kahneman1979}'s prospect theory offers insight into how people evaluate such
gambles. Two findings are relevant. First, people overweight small probabilities, which
helps explain the general appeal of lotteries: a tiny chance of a gain receives more
psychological weight than its objective probability warrants. Second, the value function
for gains is concave, meaning that each additional dollar matters less than the one
before it. A \$500 prize is not experienced as five times more valuable than a \$100
prize.

Applied to survey lotteries, these principles suggest that five chances to win \$100 may
be more motivating than one chance to win \$500. Five chances feels like better odds, and
the concave value function compresses the perceived difference between the two prize
amounts. The alternative prediction is that prize magnitude dominates: \$500 is a more
attention-grabbing number, and respondents may focus on what they could win rather than
their chances of winning. A third possibility is that respondents approximate expected
value despite uncertainty, in which case the two framings should perform equally. This
calculation is complicated by the fact that respondents do not know how many others will
enter the lottery.

Prior studies of lottery framing in surveys have produced mixed results. Surveys
conducted within institutional populations present a particular puzzle for incentive
research. People with strong ties to the sponsoring institution are likely already
motivated to participate. Donors, association members, and those with deeper institutional
involvement may respond at high rates without any incentive. For these individuals,
incentives may add little.

By contrast, those with weaker institutional ties may lack intrinsic motivation to
respond. According to leverage-saliency theory, incentives should matter most for this
group, because the monetary offer provides leverage that the sponsor relationship does
not. If this is correct, incentives would improve sample representativeness by increasing
participation among the weakly affiliated.

But there is an alternative possibility. Cialdini's reciprocity principle suggests that
incentives might actually amplify participation among the already-engaged. Donors, for
instance, have given to the institution. When the institution offers something in return,
donors may feel the reciprocity norm particularly strongly. If so, incentives could
increase response most among those who already respond at high rates, worsening rather
than improving representativeness.

This study examines lottery incentives in an online survey of Stanford University alumni.
We randomly assigned potential respondents to one of three conditions: no incentive, five
chances to win \$100, or one chance to win \$500. This design allows us to test three
questions: whether lottery incentives increase response rates relative to no incentive,
whether the framing of the lottery affects response, and whether incentive effects vary
across subgroups defined by institutional affiliation.

We test the following hypotheses:

\bci
\item Hypothesis 1: Lottery incentives will increase overall response rates compared to
  no incentive.
\item Hypothesis 2: The framing with more chances to win (5~$\times$~\$100) will produce
  higher response rates than the framing with a larger single prize (1~$\times$~\$500).
\item Hypothesis 3: Incentive effects will be larger among weakly affiliated subgroups
  (non-donors, non-members, graduate-only alumni), thereby improving sample
  representativeness.
\eci

These hypotheses follow logically from theory and prior research. Whether they hold in
practice is an empirical question with implications for how survey researchers should
deploy limited incentive budgets.

\section{Literature Review}
% ============================================

The existing literature on lottery incentives presents a puzzle. Lotteries offer a
cost-effective alternative to guaranteed payments, yet experimental studies reveal
substantial heterogeneity in their effectiveness. Some find large positive effects;
others find nothing at all. This review examines the sources of this heterogeneity, with
particular attention to the understudied question of whether lottery incentives affect not
just response rates but also the composition of the responding sample.

\subsection{Population Characteristics and Lottery Effectiveness}

The most consistent pattern in the literature is that lottery effectiveness varies by
population. Student and younger populations show reliable positive effects.
\textcite{laguilles2011} and \textcite{larose2014} found significant increases among
college students, as did \textcite{sanchezfernandez2010} and \textcite{porter2003}.
\textcite{martinson2000} similarly found that younger respondents were more responsive to
lottery incentives.

General population surveys also tend to show positive effects. \textcite{olsen2012},
\textcite{johansson1997}, \textcite{pedersen2016}, \textcite{woodward1985}, and
\textcite{marrett1992} all documented significant increases in response rates. Early
studies established the basic finding: \textcite{mccool1991} found a 12 percentage point
increase with a \$50 savings bond lottery, \textcite{blythe1986} observed an 11
percentage point gain among social workers, and \textcite{balakrishnan1992} reported a
6.8 point increase in a large consumer survey. International replications suggest these
effects generalize beyond U.S.\ samples \parencite{angur1995, johansson1997, Conn.etal2025}.

The pattern reverses for high-income, highly educated, and professional populations. Null
findings are common in surveys of physicians and healthcare professionals
\parencite{moses2004, cottrell2015, gravagubins2008, halpern2011, ulrich2005,
dykema2011, gallagher2001, mclaren2000, so2018}. Surveys of older adults show similar
null results \parencite{finsen2006, gjostein2016, roberts2000}, as do surveys of
professionals and executives \parencite{lorenzi1988, coryn2020, golden1981}.
\textcite{warriner1996}, \textcite{harris2008}, \textcite{lipps2010}, and
\textcite{brennan1993} found no significant differences between lottery and control
conditions. \textcite{mortagy1985} famously characterized their lottery condition as ``a
useless raffle,'' finding identical response rates (68.1\%) in both groups.

Most relevant to the present study, \textcite{denton1991} found lottery incentives
ineffective among college alumni. This population shares key characteristics with
Stanford alumni: high education, relative affluence, and an existing relationship with
the survey sponsor. \textcite{zhang2017} provides a potential explanation, finding larger
lottery effects among lower-income respondents. For affluent populations, the expected
value of a modest lottery may fall below the threshold required to motivate behavior
change.

Several studies have found that small prepaid cash incentives significantly outperform
much larger lottery prizes. \textcite{larson2003} found that a \$1 prepaid incentive
outperformed a \$600 lottery, and \textcite{lorenzi1988} found lottery incentives
performed significantly worse than \$1 prepaid cash among business executives. These
findings suggest that the certainty of a small reward may be more motivating than the
possibility of a large one, particularly for populations that do not need the money.

Recent meta-analytic evidence confirms the overall pattern. \textcite{goritz2006} found
an odds ratio of 1.19 for lottery incentives in web surveys, and
\textcite{abdelazeem2023} reported a risk ratio of 1.12 for lotteries compared to 1.25
for cash. Lotteries work on average, but with smaller and more variable effects than
guaranteed payments.

\subsection{Prize Structure and Framing Effects}

When researchers have a fixed incentive budget, they face a choice: offer many chances to
win smaller prizes, or fewer chances to win larger prizes. The expected value can be held
constant, but the psychological appeal may differ. Prospect theory suggests that people
overweight small probabilities, which could favor either framing depending on whether
respondents focus on the number of chances or the prize magnitude.

The evidence on prize structure is mixed. \textcite{deutskens2004} found that lotteries
with smaller prizes and higher probability of winning were most effective. This finding
aligns with the prediction that multiple chances feel psychologically better than a single
long-shot opportunity.

Other studies find no significant differences across lottery amounts.
\textcite{leung2002} and \textcite{halpern2011} found that varying the prize magnitude
had no effect on response rates. Some evidence suggests larger prizes are more effective
\parencite{hubbard1988, gajic2012}, but this pattern is inconsistent.

Lottery type and timing also matter. Prepaid scratch tickets appear to outperform
promised lottery entries \parencite{olsen2012, guo2016}, possibly because their
immediacy and tangibility increase perceived value. \textcite{tuten2004} found that
immediate notification of lottery results outperformed delayed notification.
Pre-incentive lotteries (unconditional entry) may be more effective than post-incentive
lotteries (conditional on completion) \parencite{sanchezfernandez2010}.

Interactions with survey characteristics add further complexity. \textcite{spry1989}
found lottery incentives helped only when combined with a short questionnaire, suggesting
that incentive effectiveness depends on respondent burden. \textcite{cobanoglu2003} found
that lottery combined with other incentive types was effective, while lottery alone was
not.

\subsection{The Understudied Question: Sample Composition}

Most studies of lottery incentives focus exclusively on response rates. A higher response
rate is treated as a success. This framing ignores a critical question: do incentives
change \emph{who} responds? If incentives motivate participation uniformly across the
population, higher response rates translate directly into better data. If incentives
disproportionately attract certain subgroups, they may increase response rates while
simultaneously reducing representativeness. A survey with a higher response rate could
have more nonresponse bias than one with a lower response rate, depending on which
additional respondents the incentive recruits.

Several studies provide indirect evidence of differential effects. \textcite{laguilles2011}
observed that males showed larger response increases for technology prizes while females
responded more to dining-related prizes. \textcite{zhang2017} found larger lottery
effects among low-income staff compared to higher-income staff. These patterns suggest
incentives do not operate uniformly across demographic groups.

A few studies have examined composition directly. \textcite{robertson2005} found that a
lottery incentive improved physician response rates and examined representativeness of
responders versus nonresponders. \textcite{petrolia2009} found that incentives altered
the education composition of their sample. \textcite{goyder1994} found that prepaid
incentives yielded a more representative sample overall.

These studies raise the possibility that incentives could improve representativeness by
drawing in groups that would otherwise be underrepresented. Lower-income respondents, for
whom the expected value of a lottery represents a larger relative payoff, might be
particularly motivated by financial incentives. If low-income individuals are
underrepresented at baseline, incentives could reduce nonresponse bias.

The alternative possibility has received less empirical attention. Incentives might
increase response most among groups already inclined to participate. In institutional
populations like alumni, donors and active members may feel the reciprocity norm
particularly strongly: they have given to the institution, and now the institution offers
to give something back. If incentives disproportionately motivate the already-engaged,
they would widen existing gaps in response propensity, increasing nonresponse bias
despite increasing the overall response rate.

\section{Methods}
% ============================================

\subsection{Sample and Assignment}

The population was defined as people who received degrees from Stanford University
between 1955 and 2007, who live in the continental USA or Canada, and for whom Stanford
had an e-mail address. Stanford employees and their spouses, current and former
University Trustees, current and former members of the Stanford Alumni Association Board
of Directors, and those who had requested to receive no e-mail were excluded from the
study.

Alumni were assigned to experimental conditions using systematic sampling. After removing
those who had requested to receive no surveys ($N = 2{,}499$, $2{,}513$, and $2{,}510$
from the three groups), invitations were distributed sequentially in a 1-2-3-1-2-3
pattern, yielding three groups of alumni. Each individual was e-mailed a short invitation
with a link to participate in the survey. The invitation was the same for all---except
for the subject headers and the passages describing the incentive offered for their
participation.

\bci
\item Sample 1 was offered no incentive to participate in the survey.
\item Sample 2 was informed that five randomly selected respondents would each win a
  \$100 Visa gift card.
\item Sample 3 was informed that one randomly selected respondent would win a \$500 Visa
  gift card.
\eci

The invitations were e-mailed on June 24, 2008, and three reminders were e-mailed to
non-respondents at one-week intervals before the survey was taken off the web on July 20,
2008. A few invitations bounced back as undeliverable ($N = 22$, $29$, and $24$,
respectively), yielding final participating samples of 2,430, 2,449, and 2,454. A
balance check confirmed that the three experimental groups did not differ significantly
on any measured demographic characteristic (all $p > 0.16$), validating the
effectiveness of the systematic assignment procedure.

\subsection{Measures}

Names and demographic data came from Stanford's alumni database. The following
characteristics served as covariates in the response rate models, capturing the
dimensions along which response propensity varied in this population. Reference
categories are noted in parentheses.

\emph{Sex.} Male (reference) or female.

\emph{Donor status.} Whether the alumnus had donated to Stanford since January 1, 2007
(reference: non-donor).

\emph{SAA membership.} Whether the alumnus was a member of the Stanford Alumni
Association at the time of the survey (reference: non-member).

\emph{Degree level.} Alumni whose only degree from Stanford is a graduate degree
(``graduate-only,'' reference) versus those who hold an undergraduate degree from
Stanford (``undergraduate''). Alumni who have both undergraduate and graduate degrees
from Stanford are classified as undergraduate alumni.

\emph{Graduation year cohort.} Graduation years grouped into five cohorts: 1955--1969
(reference), 1970--1979, 1980--1989, 1990--1999, and 2000--2007. Year of undergraduate
graduation is recorded for alumni who have both undergraduate and graduate degrees.

\emph{Geographic region.} San Francisco Bay Area (reference), other active alumni
regions, or non-active regions.

The response rate analyses (Analyses 1--3) draw on a dataset covering all 7,333 sample
members, which includes treatment assignment, response status, and the demographic
characteristics listed above. Age and race/ethnicity were not available in this file.
Among the 2,342 respondents, a separate line-item file records responses to each survey
item along with self-reported age and race/ethnicity. Age (D1) was recorded as a
continuous measure in years (range: 21--88). Race/ethnicity (D2) was recorded using
seven categories: White/Caucasian (reference), Asian/Pacific Islander/Indian,
Latino/Hispanic, African-American/Black, Native American/American Indian, Mixed
ethnicity/multiracial/other, and Prefer not to answer. These two variables are used as
additional controls in the data quality analyses (Analysis 4) but are not available for
the full sample.

\emph{Survey instrument.} The survey contained two batteries of attitudinal items. The
Q1 battery comprised 26 items asking alumni to rate how much each activity made them feel
connected to Stanford on a six-point scale (1 = a great deal, 2 = a lot, 3 = a moderate
amount, 4 = only a little, 5 = not at all, 6 = not applicable). The Q2 battery comprised
16 items asking alumni to rate the extent to which each alumni service met their needs or
interests (1 = fully, 2 = mostly, 3 = partly, 4 = only a little, 5 = not at all, 6 = not
applicable). For response-quality analyses, ``not applicable'' responses (6) were treated
as missing, as they represent inapplicability rather than a position on the attitude
continuum.

\subsection{Analysis Plan}

We conducted four sets of analyses. The estimating equations and full statistical output
for each are presented in the Analysis section; interpretive findings follow in the
Results section.

\emph{Analysis 1: Overall incentive effect.} We compared response rates across the three
experimental conditions using chi-square tests and one-sided tests (Fisher's exact and
one-sided $z$-test) given our directional hypothesis. We then estimated bivariate and
multivariate OLS regression models predicting survey response (0/1) as a function of
treatment assignment and demographic covariates. We conducted joint Wald tests of the
null hypothesis that $\beta_1 = 0$. The bivariate model takes the form:

\begin{equation}
  \text{Responded}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{AnyIncentive}_i + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

The multivariate model adds the full covariate vector $\mathbf{X}_i$ described in
Section~3.2:

\begin{equation}
  \text{Responded}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{AnyIncentive}_i +
  \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

\emph{Analysis 2: Incentive framing.} To test whether lottery structure matters when
expected value is held constant, we restricted the sample to the two incentive conditions
(excluding the no-incentive control) and coded the treatment variable as 1 = one \$500
prize, 0 = five \$100 prizes (reference). We first compared raw response rates via
chi-square test:

\begin{equation}
  \text{Responded}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{One500}_i + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

We then estimated multivariate models adding $\mathbf{X}_i$, and tested for
heterogeneous framing effects by adding treatment-by-covariate interaction terms with
joint Wald tests:

\begin{equation}
  \text{Responded}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{One500}_i +
  \mathbf{X}_i'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \epsilon_i
\end{equation}

\emph{Analysis 3: Subgroup effects.} We examined differential incentive effects across
demographic subgroups through two complementary approaches: two-proportion $z$-tests
comparing no-incentive and pooled-incentive response rates within each subgroup, and
interaction models using the full sample with treatment-by-demographic terms and joint
Wald tests.

\emph{Analysis 4: Data quality.} Among respondents ($N = 2{,}342$: 781 no-incentive,
1,561 incentive), we assessed response quality using five indicators: (1) item
nonresponse---percentage of questions left unanswered per respondent, compared via
$t$-test and multivariate OLS controlling for age and race/ethnicity; (2)
straight-lining---identical responses across 10 or more Q1 items or 8 or more Q2 items,
compared via chi-square test; (3) within-person response variance---standard deviation
across answered items per battery, compared via $t$-test; (4) Mulligan
non-differentiation scores---a pairwise-difference measure of response uniformity robust
to scale endpoints \parencite{anand2013}, estimated via $t$-test and multivariate OLS
controlling for age and race/ethnicity; and (5) response distributions---chi-square tests
on all 42 survey items comparing response distributions between conditions, with
Bonferroni--Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

\section{Analysis}
% ============================================

\subsection{Baseline Response Rates}

A total of 2,342 out of 7,333 alumni participated in the survey, yielding an overall
response rate of 31.9\%. Response rates varied substantially across demographic and
affiliation characteristics. Alumni with stronger institutional ties showed markedly
higher participation: donors responded at 40.6\% compared to 26.5\% among non-donors
($\chi^2 = 157.86$, $p < 0.001$); SAA members at 36.4\% versus 26.3\% among non-members
($\chi^2 = 84.59$, $p < 0.001$); and undergraduate alumni at 35.0\% compared to 27.7\%
among graduate-only alumni ($\chi^2 = 43.79$, $p < 0.001$). Women responded at
significantly higher rates (35.9\%) than men (29.5\%) ($\chi^2 = 32.00$, $p < 0.001$).
Among undergraduate alumni, response rates varied by graduation year cohort, with those
who graduated in the 1970s showing the highest participation (38.3\%) and those in the
1980s and 1990s showing lower rates (32.6\% and 32.3\%, respectively)
($\chi^2 = 11.38$, $p < 0.01$).

\subsection{Analysis 1: Overall Incentive Effect on Response Rates}

Alumni offered any lottery incentive responded at 32.47\%, compared to 30.62\% in the
no-incentive condition---a raw difference of 1.85 percentage points. A one-sided $z$-test
yielded $p = 0.055$, a one-tailed Fisher's exact test $p = 0.057$, and a two-sided
chi-square test $p = 0.109$.

In bivariate OLS models, the estimated incentive effect was 1.85 percentage points
($b = 0.019$, $\text{SE} = 0.011$, $p = 0.107$). Adding demographic controls increased
the point estimate slightly to 2.01 percentage points while reducing the $p$-value
($b = 0.020$, $\text{SE} = 0.011$, $p = 0.076$). This pattern---where controls reduce
the $p$-value rather than increase it---indicates that the demographic covariates are
negatively correlated with treatment assignment, increasing statistical precision upon
their inclusion.

The strongest predictors in the multivariate model were institutional affiliation
measures. Donors were 12.0 percentage points more likely to respond than non-donors
($p < 0.01$); SAA members were 6.7 points more likely than non-members ($p < 0.01$);
women were 5.8 points more likely than men ($p < 0.01$); and undergraduate alumni were
2.5 points more likely than graduate-only alumni ($p = 0.033$). Among graduation year
cohorts, the 1990s cohort showed 4.9 points lower response than the 1955--1969 reference
cohort ($p = 0.004$). All institutional affiliation effects substantially exceeded the
incentive effect in magnitude.

\subsection{Analysis 2: Effect of Lottery Structure on Response Rates}

The two incentive conditions---five chances to win \$100 versus one chance to win
\$500---had identical expected values (\$2.00 per respondent). Empirically, the two
framings produced virtually identical response rates: 32.58\% for five \$100 prizes
versus 32.36\% for one \$500 prize, a difference of 0.22 percentage points
($\chi^2 = 0.03$, $p = 0.864$).

Multivariate OLS models comparing the two incentive conditions directly confirmed this
null result ($F = 0.04$, $p = 0.836$), with point estimates differing by less than 0.3
percentage points from the bivariate specification.

\subsection{Analysis 3: Heterogeneous Treatment Effects Across Demographic Subgroups}

Two-proportion $z$-tests comparing no-incentive and pooled-incentive response rates
within each subgroup revealed a consistent pattern: incentive effects were largest among
groups with the highest baseline response rates. Women showed a 3.4 percentage point
increase ($\chi^2 = 3.15$, $p = 0.076$), while men showed only 0.9 points
($\chi^2 = 0.38$, $p = 0.539$). Donors increased by 3.4 points ($\chi^2 = 2.96$,
$p = 0.085$), while non-donors increased by 1.0 point ($\chi^2 = 0.46$, $p = 0.498$).

\input{tables/XXXX_tab5}

Formal interaction models including treatment-by-demographic terms found no individual
interaction to be statistically significant. The largest point estimates---for donors
($\hat{\gamma} = 0.031$, $p = 0.208$) and women ($\hat{\gamma} = 0.027$,
$p = 0.267$)---were directionally consistent with the $z$-test patterns but well below
conventional thresholds. Joint Wald tests failed to reject homogeneous treatment effects
($F = 0.67$, $p = 0.755$).

\subsection{Analysis 4: Incentive Effects on Response Quality}

\subsubsection{Item Nonresponse}

Respondents in the incentive condition left significantly fewer questions unanswered
(1.99\%) compared to the no-incentive condition (2.81\%), a difference of 0.82
percentage points ($t = 2.089$, $p = 0.037$). This represents a 29\% reduction in item
nonresponse. In multivariate OLS controlling for age and race/ethnicity, the incentive
coefficient was $b = -0.823$, $\text{SE} = 0.393$, $p = 0.037$ ($N = 2{,}342$;
$R^2 = 0.014$). The effect persisted after accounting for demographic composition.

Five items showed particularly high nonresponse across both conditions: Q2O (8.07\%),
Q2P (5.47\%), Q2N (5.34\%), Q2L (4.91\%), and Q2G (4.82\%)---all from the Q2 battery,
all including a ``Not Applicable'' response option.

\subsubsection{Satisficing Behavior}

Straight-lining was virtually nonexistent in both conditions. For the Q1 battery, only 3
of 2,342 respondents (0.13\%) gave identical responses to 10 or more items, with
identical rates across conditions ($\chi^2 = 0.00$, $p = 1.000$). The Q2 battery showed
slightly higher but still minimal rates: 1.47\% in no-incentive versus 0.63\% in
incentive conditions ($\chi^2 = 2.54$, $p = 0.111$).

Within-person response variance showed no significant differences between conditions.
Mean standard deviations were nearly identical for Q1 ($1.51$ vs.\ $1.49$,
$t = -0.88$, $p = 0.380$) and Q2 ($1.67$ vs.\ $1.64$, $t = -1.25$, $p = 0.211$).
Mulligan non-differentiation scores---a pairwise-difference measure more robust to scale
endpoints than simple variance---corroborated this result: mean ND scores were virtually
identical between conditions for Q1, Q2, and all 42 items combined, with no bivariate or
multivariate test approaching significance.

\subsubsection{Response Distributions}

Chi-square tests on all 42 survey items with Bonferroni--Holm correction found no item
with a statistically significant distributional difference between conditions after
correction. The two items with the smallest unadjusted $p$-values---Q1R and Q1A, both
$p = 0.004$---had adjusted $p$-values of 0.146.

\subsection{Alumni with Stronger Institutional Ties Are Substantially More Likely to
  Respond}

A total of 2,342 out of 7,333 alumni participated in the survey, yielding an overall
response rate of 31.9\%.\footnote{People who had asked to receive no surveys were removed
  from the three random samples ($N = 2{,}499$, $2{,}513$, and $2{,}510$), leaving
  samples of 2,452, 2,478, and 2,478. Invitations were e-mailed to these people, and a
  few bounced back as undeliverable ($N = 22$, $29$, and $24$, respectively), yielding
  final participating samples of 2,430, 2,449, and 2,454.}

Before examining the experimental effects, it is important to establish who responds to
this survey without any incentive---because this baseline distribution shapes what any
incentive can and cannot accomplish. Response rates varied substantially by institutional
connection: donors responded at 40.6\% versus 26.5\% among non-donors; SAA members at
36.4\% versus 26.3\% among non-members; undergraduate alumni at 35.0\% versus 27.7\%
among graduate-only alumni. Women responded at markedly higher rates (35.9\%) than men
(29.5\%). All of these differences were highly significant (all $p < 0.001$).

These differences were consistent across all measured characteristics: donors, SAA
members, women, and undergraduate alumni all participated at substantially higher rates
than their counterparts. This baseline heterogeneity is not merely descriptive
context---it shapes what any incentive can and cannot accomplish.

\subsection{Lottery Incentives Produce a Small, Inconsistent Increase in Response Rates}

Lottery incentives increased overall response rates by approximately 2 percentage points
(30.6\% to 32.5\%), but this effect was small and statistically marginal. The most
permissive test---a one-sided $z$-test reflecting our directional hypothesis---yielded
$p = 0.055$, just above the conventional threshold. Two-sided tests and joint Wald tests
failed to reject the null of no effect.

The incentive effect's sensitivity to the inclusion of demographic controls is itself
informative. In bivariate models the effect is $p = 0.107$; once demographic controls are
added it moves to $p = 0.076$. Neither crosses the 0.05 threshold. Statistical power to
detect a 2 percentage point effect is inherently limited in a study of this size, and we
cannot rule out that a true but small effect exists. What we can say is that the evidence
for a meaningful boost in participation is weak---and that the 91 additional respondents
generated cost \$10.99 each in prize expenditure. Whether that cost is justified depends
critically on which respondents the incentives recruited, a question we take up next.

\subsection{Lottery Structure Has No Effect: Framing Does Not Matter When Expected Value
  Is Equal}

The two lottery structures---five chances at \$100 versus one chance at \$500---produced
virtually identical response rates (32.58\% and 32.36\%), a gap of 0.22 percentage
points that was nowhere near statistical significance. This null result held across all
demographic subgroups as well.

This finding has a clear practical implication for survey researchers working with
constrained budgets: when designing a lottery incentive for a similar population, the
structure of the prize is irrelevant. A single large prize and multiple smaller prizes of
equal total value are interchangeable. Respondents in this population appear to evaluate
lotteries on the basis of expected value rather than through the psychological
biases---overweighting of probabilities, sensitivity to prize magnitude---that prospect
theory might predict.

\subsection{Incentives Widen Existing Participation Gaps Rather Than Narrowing Them}

The most consequential finding of this study is not the overall response rate increase
but \emph{who} generated it. Incentives increased participation most among the groups
already most likely to respond: women ($+3.4$ pp) versus men ($+0.9$ pp), and donors
($+3.4$ pp) versus non-donors ($+1.0$ pp).

The practical implication is that incentives did not broaden the survey's reach---they
deepened participation among groups with high baseline response propensity. The response
rate difference between women and men widened from 4.7 to 7.3 percentage points, and
between donors and non-donors from 12.5 to 14.9 percentage points. Though these
differential effects did not achieve conventional statistical significance, the consistent
pattern across subgroups suggests that incentives amplified rather than reduced existing
differences in who was likely to respond.

\subsection{Data Quality Is Preserved: Incentives Improve Completion Without Biasing
  Responses}

A concern sometimes raised about financial incentives is that they attract inattentive
respondents who rush through surveys and provide low-quality answers. The evidence here
runs counter to that expectation. Incentivized respondents left \emph{fewer} questions
unanswered (1.99\% vs.\ 2.81\%), a 29\% reduction in item nonresponse that persisted
after controlling for demographics. The incentive was associated with more thorough
completion of the survey, not less.

Beyond item nonresponse, every other quality indicator showed no significant difference
by condition. Straight-lining was negligible and equal across groups. Within-person
response variance was nearly identical. Response distributions on all 42 substantive
items showed no significant differences after multiple-comparison correction. Incentivized
and non-incentivized alumni evaluated their Stanford connection and satisfaction with
alumni services in statistically indistinguishable ways.

This is an important reassurance: whatever compositional effects incentives introduced,
they did not corrupt the substantive answers of those who responded. The concern is not
that incentivized respondents gave worse data---it is that incentives recruited more of
the same kinds of respondents who would have participated anyway.


% ============================================
\section{Discussion}
% ============================================

\subsection{Summary of Main Findings}

Three principal findings emerged from this experiment. First, lottery incentives produced
a small and inconsistent increase in response rates---approximately 2 percentage
points---that was sensitive to the inclusion of demographic controls ($p$-values ranging
from 0.055 to 0.107) and failed to achieve significance under joint Wald tests
($p \approx 0.20$). The 91 additional responses generated came at a total prize cost of
\$1,000, a marginal cost of \$10.99 per additional respondent. Second, incentive framing
had no detectable effect when expected value was held constant: five chances at \$100
produced virtually identical response rates as one chance at \$500 (difference = 0.22
percentage points, $p = 0.864$). Third, incentives disproportionately increased response
among subgroups already over-represented at baseline---particularly women and
donors---amplifying rather than reducing existing differences in who was likely to
respond.

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

\subsubsection{Population Characteristics and Lottery Effectiveness}

The weak incentive effect is consistent with prior evidence that lotteries perform poorly
in affluent, highly educated, and institutionally connected populations
\parencite{cottrell2015, denton1991}. For Stanford alumni, the \$2 expected value likely
falls well below any motivational threshold. The pre-existing relationship between alumni
and the sponsor may further reduce the marginal impact of a financial offer, as
institutional affiliation already provides substantial leverage for many potential
respondents.

\subsubsection{Prize Structure and Framing Effects}

The null framing effect suggests that this population evaluated the two lotteries on the
basis of expected value rather than through the heuristic processing that prospect theory
might predict. The explicit disclosure of prize amounts and number of winners likely
facilitated rational calculation of expected value, and a highly educated population may
be particularly capable of doing so. This contrasts with \textcite{deutskens2004}, who
found higher-probability, smaller-prize lotteries more effective---a discrepancy that may
reflect differences in population affluence or the absolute magnitude of the prizes
offered. Practically, researchers designing lottery incentives for similar populations
need not devote resources to optimizing the probability-prize combination.

\subsubsection{Respondent Composition and Nonresponse Bias}

The differential effects across subgroups speak directly to the understudied question of
whether lottery incentives change \emph{who} responds, not just \emph{how many} respond.
Rather than broadening participation among underrepresented alumni, incentives
concentrated additional responses among those already most likely to participate. Two
theoretical accounts are consistent with this pattern. Leverage-saliency theory
\parencite{groves2000} suggests that the monetary offer was simply more salient to
respondents already engaged with the institution. The norm of reciprocity offers a
complementary explanation: donors, having already given to Stanford, may feel the
incentive activates an obligation of reciprocal exchange that non-donors do not share.
Either way, the consistent directional pattern suggests that incentives operated through
mechanisms tied to existing institutional engagement rather than compensating for its
absence.

\subsection{Methodological Implications}

These findings suggest that lottery incentives warrant careful consideration in
institutional survey contexts. Cost per additional response is an incomplete metric when
incentives disproportionately attract respondents who are already over-represented: the
cost per additional representative response may substantially exceed \$10.99. Researchers
should assess baseline heterogeneity in response propensity before deploying incentives,
and consider whether alternative strategies---targeted outreach to underrepresented
groups, prepaid incentives \parencite{larson2003}, or additional contact
attempts---would better serve representativeness goals without differential effects.
Because all conditions in this study received identical follow-up protocols, we cannot
assess whether incentives reduce the optimal number of reminders needed---a potential
cost offset that warrants investigation in future designs that manipulate both incentives
and follow-up intensity \parencite{olsen2012}. Evaluations of incentive effectiveness
should routinely examine the demographic breakdown of respondents across conditions, not
merely overall response rates.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. The Stanford alumni population is more
educated, affluent, and institutionally connected than most survey populations, which
limits generalizability; incentive effects may be larger, more uniform, or differently
distributed in less engaged or lower-income samples. The modest expected value (\$2 per
respondent) may fall below effectiveness thresholds that larger prizes would cross, and
dose-response questions remain open. The reciprocity mechanism we hypothesize for donors
was not directly tested and would benefit from experimental manipulation---for instance,
by explicitly referencing past donations in incentive messaging and comparing responses
to those with no prior giving history. Finally, this study examined a single survey wave;
whether incentives create expectations that depress future response in their absence is
unknown.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Lottery incentives produced a small and inconsistent increase in response rates while
disproportionately motivating subgroups already over-represented among respondents,
widening existing gaps in participation. Data quality was preserved---incentivized
respondents completed the survey more thoroughly and answered substantive questions in
statistically indistinguishable ways---but this reassurance does not address the
compositional concern. When incentives differentially attract high-propensity
respondents, they can increase nonresponse bias even as they increase total response.

For researchers designing surveys within institutional populations---alumni, employees,
members---the practical recommendation is clear: before deploying a lottery incentive,
assess who is already likely to respond and whether the incentive will reach those who
are not. If baseline response propensity is heterogeneous and strongly correlated with
the survey's substantive variables, a lottery incentive may deliver a marginal increase
in total response while widening existing differences in who participates. The
appropriate benchmark for evaluating incentive strategies is not response rate alone, but
whether the additional responses improve the representativeness and analytical value of
the data.
